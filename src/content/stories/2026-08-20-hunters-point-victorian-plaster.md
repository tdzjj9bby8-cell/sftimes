Ask most people what San Francisco looks like and they will tell you about the Golden Gate Bridge, or the Transamerica Pyramid. Lorna Kollmeyer would let you get that far, and then correct you.

"But then you think of the Victorians," she has said. "San Francisco is just the motherlode of Victorian architecture. People come from far and wide to see them. It's just one of the biggest identities of our city."

The painted ladies are the postcard. The flamboyant, almost garish plaster that bejewels them, the scrolls and rosettes and cartouches, the life-size fruit and the floral swags, the faces of Egyptian pharaohs and Greek warriors, is the part almost nobody thinks about until a piece of it rots off the front of a hundred-year-old house and has to be replaced. Once, a number of shops around the city could make those pieces again. Today there is one. It sits in a leaking building at Hunters Point Shipyard, and it is run by a former All-American basketball player who never set out to become the guardian of the city's ornamental memory.

## A business she was simply offered

Kollmeyer did not train for this. She was a collegiate basketball player with a liberal arts degree who spent a year playing professionally in Avignon, France. When a visit to the Bay Area redirected her life, she pieced together a living the way many young people in San Francisco once could: she organized athletic events like Bay to Breakers, coached basketball at Balboa High School and at the JCC, and took carpentry jobs to cover the rent. For a while she worked as a "gal Friday" for a couple, Peter and Helene Marchant, who restored and flipped neglected Victorians. That was where she first saw plaster casting done.

One day, she remembers, Marchant turned to her and asked whether she knew anyone who wanted to buy a plaster business.

"I thought, this is pretty cool," she has recalled. "I'll do it."

She is candid that there was no grand design. "This wasn't part of a master plan I had creatively come up with," she told Cabana Magazine. "It's the result of my passive decisions with a series of jobs life offered me." The shop was born, by one account, out of a basement on Broderick Street. Her first major commission was the restoration of the facade of the turn-of-the-century Hotel Majestic, at the corner of Sutter and Gough. She put in what felt to her like an enormous bid, $23,000, and learned only later that it was roughly $30,000 under her nearest competitor. She got the job. In 1984 she moved into her first studio at Hunters Point. The rent was $148 a month.

Four decades later, the shop she took over almost by accident, Lorna Kollmeyer Ornamental Plaster, is the last of its kind in San Francisco, and the single largest surviving archive of the city's decorative plaster.

## How you save a rosette

The work itself is unglamorous and exacting. When a homeowner or a restoration contractor arrives with a piece that is rotting off a building or crumbling past repair, the process is always the same. "We take the piece, take the paint off of it, repair it, and make a mold of it," Kollmeyer has explained. "Then the molds that we make we keep in the archive."

That last step is the quiet, consequential one. Every job leaves behind a rubber mold, and every mold is kept. Over 40 years those molds have accumulated into shelves of the city's architectural DNA, a physical index of ornament that can be pulled and recast whenever the same detail is needed again somewhere else. By Kollmeyer's own estimate, somewhere around 60 to 70 percent of all of San Francisco's Victorian plaster ornamentation now lives, in negative, in her studio.

San Francisco's ornament is its own dialect. The city's ceiling medallions, she notes, are "very different from the East Coast and Midwest medallions, which are more classical and Georgian." Where the East Coast tends toward restraint, San Francisco tends toward exuberance. The studio's walls carry the evidence: corbels, plaques and brackets, cartouches and scrolls, reliefs of lions and cherubs and mythological faces, some of them inspired by a visit to Sir John Soane's Museum in London. The shop's work is not confined to house facades, either. Kollmeyer's team has cast a scroll for the Fox Theater in Redwood City, restored a Forest Hill home's eaves in the style of the architect Louis Sullivan, and sent work as far as Tokyo. She runs the studio now with two partners, Mike Dyar and Amy Firman.

## The sword over the shipyard

The threat to all of this is not a lack of demand. It is rent, and time.

Hunters Point Shipyard has been, for 40 years, one of the last affordable places in the city where a person can keep a working studio. That is precisely what has kept Kollmeyer's archive intact and in San Francisco. It is also what is now in question. The buildings are aging around their tenants. Windows break out. Roofs leak. Redevelopment has been threatened, deferred, and threatened again for years.

"The sword of Damocles gets lowered and raised," Kollmeyer has said of the long limbo over the Shipyard's future. Her buildings have been slated for demolition, then reprieved, perhaps for another 10 or 15 or 20 years. What the businesses out there cannot survive, she has warned, is a sudden tripling or quadrupling of their rent. The math that made the archive possible does not work at market rate.

The deeper problem is one of succession. Kollmeyer belongs to Artistic License, a guild of Bay Area artisans devoted to the city's historic buildings, and she has watched that guild age. "We're on the verge of losing this incredibly valuable brain trust," she has said. The artisans at the Shipyard have a shared, unfunded dream of turning the property into a vocational center that would train a new generation to protect San Francisco's architectural heritage. Until something like that exists, the responsibility, at least for plaster, sits with three people in one leaking building.

There is a plain irony in it. A city whose identity is built on its Victorians has left the survival of its Victorian ornament to a single shop it has never quite figured out how to protect. If Lorna Kollmeyer Ornamental Plaster is priced out, the archive does not simply move. The molds are heavy, specific, and irreplaceable, and the knowledge of how to use them is not written down anywhere but in the hands of the people who made them.

Kollmeyer, for her part, frames it less as a loss to be mourned than as a thing still worth keeping. "Maintaining the Victorians," she has said, "is a link to this fanciful, impractical, eccentric identity that San Francisco has. Our survival is going to be a win-win for everybody."

And she is still, four decades in, thinking about what happens after her. "The Hunters Point angels have been on our shoulders by affording us the space to work in and house this collection," she told Cabana Magazine. "My hope is to continue the legacy and pass the craft on to future generations."
